Stubbornness and Social Power in Opinion Dynamics:
the French–Harary–DeGroot and Friedkin–Johnsen Models
Leonardo Luigi Pepe
November 2026
Abstract
A stubborn agent, which never changes its opinion, is the extreme case of two different mechanisms of resistance to social influence: a self-weight in the French–Harary–DeGroot (FDG) model
and an attachment to a fixed prejudice in the Friedkin–Johnsen (FJ) model. Following the tutorial
by Proskurnikov and Tempo, this report compares the two models on the same small networks and
studies how these mechanisms affect convergence, the final opinions and the influence of each agent,
measured by the social power and by the influence centrality. A self-weight acts as inertia: it ties
an agent to its current opinion, which still follows the others, and on a strongly connected network
it does not prevent a consensus. A prejudice acts as an anchor: it ties an agent to a fixed opinion;
when every agent is influenced by a prejudiced agent, the FJ model is asymptotically stable on any
network, and the group agrees only if all the prejudiced agents share the same prejudice. Writing
the FJ model as an FDG model on an augmented graph, with the prejudices as virtual stubborn
agents, shows that the two models differ in what is a state: the opinion of a stubborn agent is part
of the state in the FDG model and an external input in the FJ model.

Introduction

How do the opinions of a group evolve when its members listen to each other, and what changes when
some of them resist? Models of opinion dynamics describe a social network as a weighted digraph,
whose nodes are the agents and whose edges carry the influence of one agent on another, and update
the opinions by repeated averaging. This report follows the tutorial by Proskurnikov and Tempo [5],
the course slides [2, 3, 1] and, as a support, the book by Bullo [4].
The historical account of [5] explains why the report compares two models. The first model of this
kind was proposed by French in 1956: every agent replaces its opinion with the mean of the opinions
displayed to it, and French used it to measure the social power of each agent, i.e. its ability to influence
the common opinion of the group; his conditions for consensus were proved rigorously by Harary in
1959 and 1965. In 1974 DeGroot extended the model to an arbitrary stochastic matrix, as a procedure
to reach a consensus of subjective probabilities: this is the French–Harary–DeGroot (FDG) model. In
1964 Abelson had proposed its continuous-time counterpart and observed that consensus, the outcome
of a very broad class of such models, is too strong to be realistic: what must be assumed to explain
the persistent disagreement observed in real groups? This question, the community cleavage problem,
points to agents that resist the others. In 1968 Taylor extended Abelson’s model with constant external
opinions, the prejudices, to which the agents remain attached; its discrete-time counterpart came only
in the 1990s, with the Friedkin–Johnsen (FJ) model, in which every agent mixes the average of the
opinions it listens to with a fixed prejudice, and which has been validated experimentally on small and
medium-size groups.
The thread of the report is stubbornness: an agent that never changes its opinion, and the graded
mechanisms of which it is the extreme case, a self-weight in the FDG model and a prejudice in the FJ
model. We ask how these mechanisms change three things: whether the opinions converge, what they
converge to, and how much each agent weighs in the result, measured by the social power in the FDG
model and by the influence centrality in the FJ model. The two models are compared on the same
small networks and with the same resisting agents, agent 1 in most examples, so that every difference
comes from the model and not from the data; an auxiliary network, used only for the FJ model, shows
what happens when a prejudice does not reach every agent. The continuous-time models of Abelson
and Taylor are mentioned only as an overview and are not studied in this report.
Section 1.1 recalls the notions on graphs and non-negative matrices used throughout. Section 2
introduces the FDG model and studies stubbornness and social power in it; Section 3 does the same
for the FJ model, from its convergence and the augmented graph to the influence centrality; Section 4
compares the two models.

1.1

Preliminaries

This subsection collects the notions on graphs and non-negative matrices used for both models, following the course slides [2, 3, 1].
1.1.1

Graphs

A weighted digraph G = (V, E, {aij }(i,j)∈E ) has node set V = {1, 2, . . . , n}, edge set E ⊆ V × V and a
weight aij > 0 for each edge (i, j) ∈ E; an edge (i, i) is a self-loop. Throughout the report the nodes
are agents, and an edge (i, j) means that agent i is influenced by agent j. This is the convention of the
course slides [1]; the tutorial by Proskurnikov and Tempo draws the edges in the opposite direction [5].
Definition 1.1 (Weighted adjacency matrix [2]). Given a weighted digraph G = (V, E, {aij }(i,j)∈E )
of order n the (weighted) adjacency matrix of G is the n × n matrix A in which:
(i) for each edge (i, j) ∈ E the (i, j) entry of A is equal to the edge weight aij ,
(ii) all other entries of A are equal to zero.
A digraph is strongly connected if there is a directed walk from any node to any other node, and a
node is globally reachable if it can be reached from any other node through a directed walk [2].
With our convention, a directed walk from i to j means that agent i is influenced by agent j, either
directly or through intermediaries. Hence G is strongly connected when every agent influences every
other agent, at least indirectly, and a globally reachable node is an agent that influences the whole
group.
2

Definition 1.2 (Periodic digraph [2]). A strongly connected digraph G = (V, E) is periodic if there
exists an integer k > 1 (the period) that divides the length of every simple cycle of the digraph.
A strongly connected digraph that is not periodic is called aperiodic; in particular, a strongly
connected digraph with a self-loop is aperiodic, since the self-loop is a cycle of length 1.
Definition 1.3 (Strongly connected component [1]). Given a (weighted) digraph G. A subgraph S is
a strongly connected component of G if S is strongly connected and any other subgraph of G strictly
containing S is not strongly connected.
Definition 1.4 (Condensation digraph [1]). The condensation digraph C(G) of a (weighted) digraph
G is obtained by collapsing each strongly connected component of G into a single node. For any two
distinct strongly connected components Sk and Sh there is an edge (Sk , Sh ) in C(G) iff there exists an
edge (i, j) in G with i ∈ Sk and j ∈ Sh .
A node with no outgoing edges is called a sink [2]. A sink of C(G) is a group of agents that are
influenced only by members of the group itself: no agent outside the group can change their opinions.
If G contains a globally reachable node, then C(G) has a unique sink [1].
1.1.2

Matrices

Throughout, 1n is the vector of n ones, ei the i-th vector of the canonical basis, spec(A) the set of
eigenvalues of a matrix A, and inequalities between vectors or matrices hold entrywise.
Definition 1.5 (Non-negative and stochastic matrices [3]). A matrix A = [aij ] ∈ Rn×n is
(i) non-negative (positive) if aij ≥ 0 (aij > 0, resp.) for all i, j ∈ {1, . . . , n};
(ii) row-stochastic if it is non-negative and A1n = 1n ;
(iii) column-stochastic if it is non-negative and A⊤ 1n = 1n ;
(iv) doubly-stochastic if it is row- and column-stochastic.

(i) semi-convergent if limk→+∞ Ak exists (finite);

Disagreement

The final opinions can be described group by group. The agents of a sink are not influenced by

Stubbornness and social power in the FDG model

of agent i. This section studies how self-weights, and stubborn agents in particular, affect the two
aspects introduced in Section 2.1: whether the group reaches an agreement, and how the social power

2.2.1

Modelling stubbornness: self-weights and stubborn agents

The self-weight aii is the weight that agent i gives to its own opinion [4], i.e. to its current opinion
xi (k) in (1); it indicates how open the agent is to the opinions of the others [5]. An agent with aii = 0
is open-minded and relies completely on the others; an agent with a positive self-weight changes its
opinion more slowly, but it still moves towards the opinions it listens to. In social terms, a self-weight
is a form of inertia, not an anchor to a fixed opinion.
Definition 2.5 (Stubbornness [5]). An agent is said to be stubborn if its opinion remains unchanged
independent of the others’ opinions.
If the opinions obey the FDG model, then agent i is stubborn, xi (k) ≡ xi (0), if and only if aii = 1 [5]
(and hence aij = 0 for all j ̸= i, since A is row-stochastic).
Stubbornness is therefore the extreme case of the self-weight, and there is no intermediate degree
of it: an agent with 0 < aii < 1 is not stubborn, it only has a positive self-weight.
In the digraph, a stubborn agent has no edges towards the other agents: it is influenced by nobody,
while the agents that listen to it keep doing so. Hence it forms a strongly connected component made
of a single node, which is a sink of C(G) and is aperiodic because of its self-loop. If, in addition,
every agent is influenced by the stubborn agent i, directly or through intermediaries, then i is the
only globally reachable node, and Theorem 2.2 gives w = ei : the whole group converges to the initial
opinion of the stubborn agent (see also [5]).
To move continuously from no self-weight to stubbornness, we modify only the row of agent 1.

2.2.2

Self-weights, stubbornness and convergence

The three values of t correspond to three different cases of the Rosetta stone.
• t = 0: both digraphs are strongly connected and periodic, so neither network converges (case (ii)).
• 0 < t < 1: agent 1 still listens to its neighbours with total weight 1 − t > 0, so the digraph stays
strongly connected, and the self-loop of agent 1 is a cycle of length 1, so the digraph becomes
aperiodic. By Corollary 1.12 the matrix is primitive, and the group reaches a consensus (case (i)).
• t = 1: agent 1 is stubborn and influences every other agent through the cycle, so it is the only
globally reachable node (Section 2.2.1) and the group reaches a consensus on x1 (0) (case (iii)).

defined. This situation, in which several agents are anchored to different fixed opinions, is the starting
point of the Friedkin–Johnsen model of Section 3.

In social terms, the self-weight of the FDG model and the prejudice of the FJ model are two
different forms of resistance to influence. A self-weight ties an agent to its current opinion, which still
moves towards the others (Section 2.2.1); a prejudice ties it to a fixed opinion, which never moves.
In the original formulation of Friedkin and Johnsen the prejudices coincide with the initial opinions,
u = x(0), and the susceptibilities are coupled to the self-weights through 1 − λi = wii [5]. As in [5],
we do not impose these conditions: u and x(0) are independent, and so are Λ and W . The choice
u = x(0) will be needed in Section 3.1.4 to define the influence centrality.
3.1.2

Convergence and stability

The FJ model (4) is a linear system with the constant input u. Following [5], we say that it converges
if the limit x(∞) = limk→+∞ x(k) exists for every initial condition x(0), and that it is asymptotically
stable if ΛW is convergent, i.e. ρ(ΛW ) < 1 (Definition 1.7 and Theorem 1.8). Regular matrices are
recalled in Definition 1.9.
Both properties are characterized by distinguishing the agents that are reached by the prejudices
from those that are not.
Definition 3.1 (P-dependent agents [5]). Agent i is P-dependent if it is prejudiced or influenced by
some prejudiced agent j, that is, a walk from i to j in the graph G exists. Otherwise, the agent is
P-independent.
The walk goes from the agent to the prejudiced agent because of our convention on the edges; in [5],
where the edges are drawn in the opposite direction, the same walk goes from the prejudiced agent to
the agent. In social terms, an agent is P-dependent if at least one prejudice reaches it.
A P-independent agent i is not prejudiced, so λi = 1, and it is not influenced by any P-dependent
agent: if wij > 0 with j P-dependent, there would be a walk from i to a prejudiced agent through j,

The comparison with the FDG model is instructive. In the FDG model, the consensus value w⊤ x(0)
is a weighted average of the initial opinions, with the same weights w for every agent (Theorem 2.2);
in the FJ model, the final opinions are weighted averages of the prejudices, and each agent has its own
weights, given by its row of V .
Consensus and disagreement. In the FDG model, consensus is a property of the model: the model
reaches a consensus if the final opinions coincide for any initial condition x(0) [5]. In the FJ model
with all agents P-dependent, the limit x(∞) = V u does not depend on x(0) (equation (9)): whether
the final opinions coincide is decided by the prejudices u, which are an external input, through the
matrix V , and no longer by the initial opinions. Accordingly, [5] studies the FJ model in terms of
convergence and of the form of the limit, and not in terms of consensus.

formation mechanism [5].
The relation with the social power is made precise in [5]. The convergence and the consensus of
the FDG model are equivalent, respectively, to the regularity and the full regularity of the stochastic
matrix A [5] (Definition 1.9). For a fixed W , let Λ = αIn with α ∈ [0, 1), i.e. the same susceptibility for
all agents, and consider the corresponding matrix Vα and vector cα . For α = 0, i.e. Λ = 0, every agent

keeps its initial opinion, V = I and c0 = n−1 1n : the social power is uniformly distributed between all
agents [5].
Lemma 3.5 ([5]). Let W be a fully regular matrix and w stand for the vector of French’s social power,
corresponding to the FDG model (1) with A = W . Then, w = limα→1−0 cα .
As α → 1 the agents barely listen to their prejudices and Vα = (1 − α)(I − αW )−1 → 1n w⊤ [5]:
the final opinions approach the FDG consensus, and cα → w, since in a common opinion the mean
weight of xi (0) and its weight in the consensus coincide. The limit cannot be replaced by α = 1: for
Λ = I the FJ model is the FDG model, which is not asymptotically stable, and I − W is singular since
1 ∈ spec(W ) (Theorem 1.6), so V is not defined. In social terms, the influence centrality moves from
the uniform distribution, when every agent is anchored to its own initial opinion, to French’s social
power, when the weight of the prejudices vanishes.

In the FDG model, A is primitive (Corollary 1.12) and doubly-stochastic, so the group agrees on
the average 0.5 of the initial opinions (Corollary 2.3): the self-weights slow the convergence down, but
do not prevent the consensus. In the FJ model every agent is prejudiced (Λ < I6 ), so the model is
asymptotically stable (Corollary 3.3) and x(∞) = V u, with V = 0.2 (I − 0.8 W )−1 . The matrix V is
stochastic (Theorem 3.2): every final opinion is a convex combination of the prejudices, and vij is the
weight of uj in xi (∞). All the entries of V are positive, since W is irreducible, so every prejudice enters
every final opinion, but with weights that change from row to row. The final opinions are therefore
closer to each other than the initial ones, but not equal: the prejudices differ, so by Section 3.1.3 no
consensus is possible.
This is the main difference between the two models. On a strongly connected network the FDG
model can only reach a consensus or not converge (cases (i)–(ii) of Table 1), since a disagreement
needs at least two closed groups (Theorem 2.4); the FJ model disagrees on the same network, so its
disagreement comes from the prejudices. In the augmented graph (Section 3.1.3) the six prejudices
are six stubborn virtual agents with different opinions, as in Remark 2.7. In social terms, inertia gives
way in the end, an anchor does not: a group anchored to different opinions settles on a persistent
disagreement, a cleavage of opinions in the words of [5].

Prejudices, stubbornness and influence centrality

The assumptions behind the influence centrality. The influence centrality generalizes French’s
social power [5]: wi is the share of agent i in the opinion the group agrees on, while ci tells how much
the initial opinion of agent i weighs, on average, in final opinions that may differ. The assumptions,
however, are of a different kind. The social power only needs what makes it meaningful, a consensus,
A∞ = 1n w⊤ . The influence centrality needs the two assumptions of Section 3.1.4, ρ(ΛW ) < 1 and
u = x(0), which the FJ model does not need in order to make sense. In return, with Λ = αI every
agent is a little anchored and ρ(αW ) = α < 1 for every stochastic W : cα exists on every network, also
where w does not. Since w weighs the initial state of a model that remembers x(0), and c the input of
a model that forgets it, we do not compare them directly, but in parallel.
A single anchored agent. With agent 1 as the only prejudiced agent (Figure 9), V = 16 e⊤
1 gives
c = e1 for every λ1 < 1: agent 1, however loosely anchored, collects all the influence centrality, whereas
in the FDG model its social power grows gradually with its self-weight, from 1/6 to 1 (Figure 3).
Inertia earns influence little by little, an anchor takes it all at once.
The augmented model explains this value. Section 3.1.4 observed that the augmented graph has
at least n sinks, so the social power is not defined. Here, however, the virtual agents 2′ , . . . , 6′ have
weight 1 − λi = 0: nobody listens to them, and removing them does not change the real agents. What
remains has a single sink, 1′ , aperiodic because of its self-loop and influencing every real agent through
agent 1, so it reaches a consensus (Theorem 2.2) and its social power is w = e1′ : with u = x(0), the
whole group converges to x1′ (0) = x1 (0), which is what c = e1 says. With several prejudiced agents,
as with Λ = αI below, several virtual agents are listened to, the augmented model stays in case (iv)
and has no social power: c, the mean of the rows of V , takes its place.

Conclusions

The comparison of the two models, collected in Table 3, answers the three questions of the introduction:
whether the opinions converge, what they converge to, and how much each agent weighs.
In the FDG model, a self-weight ties an agent to its current opinion. On a strongly connected
network, a single self-weight 0 < aii < 1 makes the network aperiodic, and the group reaches a
22

consensus (Figure 2); a disagreement requires at least two sinks of C(G), whatever the self-weights.
The social power of the agent grows gradually with its self-weight, from 1/6 to 1 on the networks of
Figure 1 (Figure 3).
In the FJ model, a prejudice ties an agent to a fixed opinion. When every agent is influenced
by a prejudiced agent, the model is asymptotically stable on any network, periodic ones included
(Figure 9), and its final opinions are convex combinations of the prejudices, which coincide only if all
the prejudiced agents share the same prejudice (Figures 9 and 10). A single prejudiced agent takes the
whole influence centrality; with a common susceptibility, the influence centrality tends to the social
power as the prejudices lose weight (Lemma 3.5, Figure 13).

Inertia and anchor. An agent can resist the opinions of the others in two ways, one for each model.
In the FDG model, a self-weight aii is inertia: an agent whose neighbours all hold the opinion 1 updates
as xi (k + 1) = aii xi (k) + (1 − aii ) and reaches 1, only more slowly as aii grows. In the FJ model,
an attachment 1 − λi is an anchor: the same agent updates as xi (k + 1) = λi + (1 − λi ) ui and stops
at once between 1 and its prejudice, never reaching the others. This holds for a single agent on any
network; the effects on the group are shown on the two networks of Figure 1, both strongly connected
and periodic without self-loops.
• Convergence. In the FDG model the self-weight of agent 1 is needed to make the network
aperiodic (Section 2.2); in the FJ model a single prejudiced agent that influences everybody makes
all agents P-dependent, and the model converges although the network is periodic (Corollary 3.3).
• Influence. In the FDG model, the social power of agent 1 grows gradually with its self-weight,
from 1/6 to 1 (Figure 3), and for t < 1 the consensus still depends on the initial opinions of all
agents. In the FJ model, a single anchor, even a weak one, takes the whole influence centrality,
c = e1 , and the group ends on its prejudice (Figure 9).
• Many agents. With the same weight 0.2 for every agent (Figure 10), the FDG model reaches a
consensus, as on every strongly connected network with a self-loop (Corollary 1.12), while the
FJ model keeps a disagreement as soon as the prejudices differ, on any network.
23

In social terms, inertia buys time and a gradual influence, but in the end the agent follows the group;
an anchor buys permanence: the agent never gives in completely, a single anchor takes all the influence,
and different anchors keep the group divided.
State and input. The same opinion can be a state or an input, and this decides the stability.
An FDG model with stubborn agents and its FJ rewriting [5] have the same trajectories, but the
FDG model is never asymptotically stable, while the FJ model is asymptotically stable whenever the
stubborn agents influence all the others (Corollaries 2.6 and 3.3): the opinion of a stubborn agent is a
state in the first and the input ui = xi (0) in the second. The spectra show it in one step: the stubborn
row is e⊤
i in A and zero in ΛW , so the eigenvalue 1 of the FDG model becomes 0 in the FJ model.
Conversely, the augmented model (11) turns every prejudice back into the state of a virtual agent,
adds one eigenvalue 1 for each of them, and loses the asymptotic stability.
• What is perturbed. Moving x1 (0) (Figure 7) moves a state in the FDG model, which keeps it
forever, and only the initial opinion of a real agent in the FJ model, which forgets it and returns
to the same limit V u. Moving u1 would change the FJ limit as well.
• Memory without stubbornness. A closed group of P-independent agents is a memory inside the
real agents (Figure 12): it keeps its initial opinions as a virtual agent keeps its prejudice, so the
FJ model converges without being asymptotically stable.
In social terms, what a group remembers is what the model counts as its state: the opinion of a
stubborn agent in the FDG model, the initial opinions of a closed group of P-independent agents in
the FJ model, but never the initial opinions of the agents that a prejudice reaches.
Social power and influence centrality. Both measures say how much the initial opinion of each
agent weighs in the final opinions, under the different assumptions listed in Table 3; the influence
centrality also averages over the agents, since the rows of V differ.
• A single anchor. With agent 1 the only prejudiced agent, c = e1 for every λ1 < 1, while in the
FDG model w1 grows gradually with the self-weight. The augmented model explains the value:
without the virtual agents that nobody listens to, it has a single sink 1′ , reaches a consensus and
has social power e1′ , so with a single prejudice the two measures coincide.
• Many anchors. With Λ = αI every agent is anchored, the augmented model has n sinks that are
listened to and no social power, while cα exists for every W . By Lemma 3.5, cα → w as α → 1
when W is fully regular (Figure 13): the influence centrality moves from the uniform value 1n /n,
when every agent keeps its own opinion, to the social power, when the anchors vanish.
In social terms, the social power tells who leads a group that agrees, the influence centrality who weighs
most in a group that keeps disagreeing; the second becomes the first when the anchors are released.
What is left out. In Section 3.2 the prejudices are the initial opinions, u = x(0), as in the original
theory of Friedkin and Johnsen, apart from the perturbations of x(0) in Figures 7 and 12(d), while [5]
treats u and x(0) as independent: the examples could be repeated with u ̸= x(0), for instance by
perturbing all the prejudices slightly. [5] also reads the final opinions of the FJ model as a Nash
equilibrium of a game in which each agent balances its distance from the others and from its prejudice,
and discusses the continuous-time models of Abelson and Taylor; both are beyond the scope of this
report.
