How do the opinions of a group evolve when its members listen to each other, and what
changes when some of them resist? Models of opinion dynamics describe a social network
as a weighted digraph, whose nodes are the agents and whose edges carry the influence of
one agent on another, and update the opinions by repeated averaging. This report follows
the tutorial by Proskurnikov and Tempo~\cite{proskurnikov2017tutorial}, the course
slides~\cite{baggio_graph,baggio_matrix,baggio_dt} and, as a support, the book by
Bullo~\cite{bullo2024lns}.

The historical account of~\cite{proskurnikov2017tutorial} explains why the report
compares two models. The first model of this kind was proposed by French in 1956: every
agent replaces its opinion with the mean of the opinions displayed to it, and French used
it to measure the \emph{social power} of each agent, i.e.\ its ability to influence the
common opinion of the group; his conditions for consensus were proved rigorously by
Harary in 1959 and 1965. In 1974 DeGroot extended the model to an arbitrary stochastic
matrix, as a procedure to reach a consensus of subjective probabilities: this is the
\emph{French--Harary--DeGroot} (FDG) model. In 1964 Abelson had proposed its
continuous-time counterpart and observed that consensus, the outcome of a very broad
class of such models, is too strong to be realistic: what must be assumed to explain the
persistent disagreement observed in real groups? This question, the \emph{community
cleavage problem}, points to agents that resist the others. In 1968 Taylor extended
Abelson's model with constant external opinions, the \emph{prejudices}, to which the
agents remain attached; its discrete-time counterpart came only in the 1990s, with the
\emph{Friedkin--Johnsen} (FJ) model, in which every agent mixes the average of the
opinions it listens to with a fixed prejudice, and which has been validated
experimentally on small and medium-size groups.

The thread of the report is stubbornness: an agent that never changes its opinion, and
the graded mechanisms of which it is the extreme case, a self-weight in the FDG model and
a prejudice in the FJ model. We ask how these mechanisms change three things: whether the
opinions converge, what they converge to, and how much each agent weighs in the result,
measured by the social power in the FDG model and by the influence centrality in the FJ
model. The two models are compared on the same small networks, with agent~$1$ as the
agent that resists, so that every difference comes from the model and not from the data.
The continuous-time models of Abelson and Taylor are mentioned only as an overview and
are not studied in this report.

Section~\ref{subsec:prelim} recalls the notions on graphs and non-negative matrices used

social power in it; Section~\ref{sec:fj} does the same for the FJ model, from its
convergence and the augmented graph to the influence centrality;
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} compares the two models.
