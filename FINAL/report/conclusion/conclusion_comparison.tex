The comparison of the two models, collected in Table~\ref{tab:fdg-fj}, answers the three
questions of the introduction: whether the opinions converge, what they converge to, and
how much each agent weighs.

In the FDG model, a self-weight ties an agent to its current opinion. On a strongly
connected network, a single self-weight $0 < a_{ii} < 1$ makes the network aperiodic,
and the group reaches a consensus (Figure~\ref{fig:fdg-conv}); a disagreement requires at
least two sinks of $C(G)$, whatever the self-weights. The social power of the agent grows
gradually with its self-weight, from $1/6$ to $1$ on the networks of
Figure~\ref{fig:networks} (Figure~\ref{fig:fdg-power}).

In the FJ model, a prejudice ties an agent to a fixed opinion. When every agent is
influenced by a prejudiced agent, the model is asymptotically stable on any network,
periodic ones included (Figure~\ref{fig:fj-one-prejudiced}), and its final opinions are
convex combinations of the prejudices, which coincide only if all the prejudiced agents
share the same prejudice (Figures~\ref{fig:fj-one-prejudiced}
and~\ref{fig:fj-all-prejudiced}). A single prejudiced agent takes the whole influence
centrality; with a common susceptibility, the influence centrality tends to the social
power as the prejudices lose weight (Lemma~\ref{lem:centrality-limit},
Figure~\ref{fig:fj-lemma24}).
